BuckleScript, ReScript and Melange, beside hazel-jit

Cyrus named BuckleScript and ReScript at the meeting of 2026-11-05 as technologies to know about, and perhaps to compare with, if not to use (^ticket(698)). This note compiles two programs with each of them and with hazel-jit, and puts what they make side by side: the JavaScript, how values look in it, and how fast it runs. The first is small, ^report(43)'s Petri net rule; the second is the Livelit Playground deck's largest view, the Witch slide's picture.

What the milliseconds here measure (added 11-08): a view's own work, and a small part of what follows an edit. On the Witch slide, one step of a slider shows in the picture 5.3 to 8.8 s later (317 to 526 frames at 60 fps), compiled or not, in two runs of four edits each way. The picture's view is 3.0 ms of that compiled (0.04%), and 87 ms on the interpreter (1.1%). Nearly all the rest is the program's run in Hazel's worker, 1.2 to 2.6 s in its evaluator, and its answer coming back, 4.4 to 5.4 MB, made in the worker and decoded on the main thread: a small edit and where its time goes. (Matthew, 2026-11-08: the numbers here "must only be measuring a small fraction of the total work, since they are on the order of ms, not sec".)

Outline#

  1. What each is: ReScript is BuckleScript renamed and grown into its own language; Melange is the fork that kept OCaml; js_of_ocaml, which builds Hazel, works another way.
  2. How fast: on the rule, Melange and ReScript beat JS written by hand; hazel-jit is about 2.9 times ReScript, and 225 times faster than the interpreter.
  3. The same rule, compiled: side by side.
  4. How each lays out values: lists, records, constructors, options, arguments.
  5. A view: the Witch slide's picture: all five within 15% of one another, 0.17 to 0.19 ms (about 5,200 to 6,000 fps), since most of the work is one builtin; in the Hazel test a frame is 155 ms (6 fps) on the interpreter and 3.4 ms (290 fps) compiled; in the app, in Firefox, a render is 3.0 ms compiled (330 fps) against 87 ms on the interpreter, once a drawing before the program's run stopped giving up on the definition. These are the view's own times: an edit on the slide shows in the picture seconds later, and the view is 0.04% of that (a small edit). The drawings, and one page per compiled output.
  6. ^report(45)'s ideas, here: the view's answer is drawn straight from its JS, never made Hazel; no diffs, since a view's answer never comes back.
  7. A small edit: one slider step shows in the picture 5.3 to 8.5 s later, compiled or not; the program's run in the worker and its answer coming back are nearly all of it, the picture's view 0.04% of it.
  8. Where a small edit's time goes: the evaluator is 1.2 to 2.6 s of it; most of the rest is the worker's answer, 4.4 to 5.4 MB for a 10 KB request, made in the worker and decoded on the main thread, 1.9 to 3.1 s with the page frozen.
  9. Better numbers, from ^report(45)'s sharing: after such an edit the view could redo only the changed shapes, 5 times less work, if the trip in shares what did not change.
  10. How each lays out the view's values: shapes, vertices, Html, numbers formatted, the walks.
  11. What holds across both: the compilers differ in calls, matches and layouts, and converge where a builtin does the work.
  12. What it suggests for hazel-jit: five changes to its output, in the order they would help.
  13. Could any of them be used: not as hazel-jit's back end; their layouts and optimizations, yes.
  14. What next.
  15. Each alternative, for and against: hazel-jit stays the base; ReScript and Melange are the models for its output; js_of_ocaml stays Hazel's own build.
  16. How this was made.

What each is#

Three compilers from one family, and one from another.

  • BuckleScript (2016, Bloomberg) compiled OCaml, and later Reason, to readable JavaScript: OCaml's front end and type checker, then its own back end from OCaml's typed intermediate language, one JS module per OCaml module.
  • ReScript is BuckleScript renamed (2020) and grown into a language of its own, with a syntax for JavaScript developers, its own standard library, and, since version 11, functions uncurried by default. Version 12.3.1 here.
  • Melange is the fork of BuckleScript's compiler that kept OCaml (and Reason) and joined OCaml's own tools: dune and opam. Its output is close to ReScript's, as the listings below show. Version 5.0.0 here, with OCaml 5.3.
  • js_of_ocaml, which builds Hazel itself, is the other family: it compiles OCaml's bytecode, a whole program, to JavaScript, with OCaml's runtime in JS around it. Values keep OCaml's memory layout (every block an array, its tag first), and it optimizes the whole program at once. Version 5.9.1 here.
  • hazel-jit (^report(45)) compiles Hazel's elaborated terms to JS source, in the browser, while the program runs, with a runtime of about 2.5 KB.

How fast#

On ^report(43)'s rule, Melange's and ReScript's JavaScript beat the JS a person wrote: 0.46 and 0.55 µs a call against 0.90. hazel-jit's takes 1.59 µs, about 2.9 times ReScript's and 1.8 times the hand-written; Hazel's interpreter takes 358 µs. Each answers the same 2,000 events (fire transition i mod 4, then the command performed on the marking), and all end on the same marking. js_of_ocaml's 0.71 µs is a best case: it compiled the timing loop and the rule as one program, and inlined the whole rule into the loop.

The hand-written handler is not the fastest because it is written the way people write JS: it copies arrays to walk them backwards ([...t.outputs].reverse()) and calls a closure for each input (every). The compilers' code allocates only what the result holds.

The same rule, compiled#

Melange and ReScript make the JavaScript a careful person would write; hazel-jit's is a direct translation, and js_of_ocaml's is OCaml's memory layout in JS. The rule's two functions, fire_at and on_event, as each compiler wrote them (the whole outputs are in this report's data):

Hazel (the example's rules/Rules.hz)

# Fire(i): fire transition i, if it can. #
# Step: fire one of those that can, chosen by the app's draw. #
let fire_at = fun (n, i) : (Net, Int) ->
  case Common.pick(n.transitions, i)
  | None => Say("there is no transition " ++ string_of_int(i), Done)
  | Some(t) =>
    if Common.enabled(n.marking, t) then Common.fire(t)
    else Say(t.name ++ " cannot fire now", Done)
  end;
let on_event = fun (n, e) : (Net, Event) ->
  case e
  | Fire(i) => fire_at(n, i)
  | Step =>
    let r = Common.ready(n.marking, n.transitions, 0) in
    let k = Common.len(r) in
    if k == 0 then Say("deadlock: nothing can fire", Done)
    else
      let j = int_of_float(n.draw *. float_of_int(k)) in
      fire_at(n, Common.nth(r, j))
  end

By hand (the example's jsHandler)

function jsHandler(net, ev) {
  const t = net.transitions[ev.tag === "Fire" ? ev.value : 0];
  const ok = t && t.inputs.every(a => net.marking[a.place] >= a.weight);
  if (!ok) return { tag: "Say", value: ["(js) cannot fire", { tag: "Done" }] };
  let k = { tag: "Done" };
  for (const a of [...t.outputs].reverse()) k = { tag: "Put", value: [a.place, a.weight, k] };
  for (const a of [...t.inputs].reverse()) k = { tag: "Take", value: [a.place, a.weight, k] };
  return { tag: "Say", value: ["(js) " + t.name, k] };
}

A view: the Witch slide's picture#

On the deck's largest view, the five versions run within 15% of one another: 0.17 to 0.19 ms (about 5,200 to 6,000 fps, as if updating the state were free), hand-written JS the fastest and hazel-jit the slowest of the five, 9% behind ReScript. Most of the work is one builtin: the picture's 1,985 numbers formatted with toFixed take 0.14 ms alone. The exception is js_of_ocaml through OCaml's Printf: 1.01 ms (990 fps), 5.3 times its time when it calls toFixed instead. All five draw the same SVG, byte for byte (26,044 characters), and it is the interpreter's drawing.

These are milliseconds of the view alone. After an edit on the slide, the picture shows it 5.3 to 8.8 s later, nearly all of it the program's run in Hazel's worker and its answer coming back (a small edit, where its time goes): how fast the view runs, in any of the five, changes little a person would see after an edit.

In the Hazel test: one frame#

Corrected 11-07, 13:28: this section first said "in the app's terms". It is the Hazel test's process, with the view handed the picture already evaluated. In the app, in Firefox, the picture's view was not compiled until 15:05 (cb560ebb86); now a render takes 3.0 ms there (below).

On the interpreter, a frame of the Witch's picture takes 155 ms (6 fps), about 9 frames' time at 60 fps. Compiled, it takes 3.4 ms (290 fps), 45 times less, leaving 13 ms of a 60 fps frame's 16.7 ms. These are timed in the Hazel test's process, the way the app runs a view. On the interpreter: the view evaluated (13.3 ms), then its command run, which evaluates the splice's value and is most of the time, then its Html drawn as virtual DOM. Compiled: the model carried into JS (0.88 ms), the view and its commands run in JS (1.16 ms), and the Html drawn straight from JS (0.64 ms).

That process holds all of Hazel, and there the same compiled view takes 6 times as long as in the benchmark's own process: 1.16 ms against 0.19 ms. The difference is not the code; it is likely the process's large heap and its garbage collection.

In Firefox, in the app#

Corrected 11-07, 15:05: this section said, at 13:28, that the picture's model reaches the view holding the splice's code. That is true only of the drawings before the program's run. Found with a diagnostic on GitHub's Firefox (branch diag/694-witch-splice): after the run, the model holds the picture itself (the picture's 133 shapes, no splice; corrected 20:30: this said 203 polygons, which was how often the word Polygon appears in the model's printed text), and the compiled path still refused it, with the old reason.

What was wrong. The slide draws the picture first from its model as written, before the program's run: the splice's code, skinned(dressed(outfit)), whose variables have no value there, so it cannot go into JS. The app treated that refusal as the definition's fault, and gave up on compiling the definition at all; after the run, with the picture's value in the model, the view never tried compiled again. Now a model that cannot go into JS is that model's fault: the definition stays compiled, and the next model goes in (HazelJitValue.call_view_why, a quick test beside it).

How it was timed. In the page itself: each livelit view the app draws leaves a way to run it again on its last model, without the app's memo of the last answer, both ways (globalThis.hazelJit.bench, ^ticket(694)), and the Firefox check (scripts/jit_slides.py --bench) loads the Witch slide fresh and times every polygon and picture view on it, each sample a loop of calls at least 25 ms long, since Firefox's clock there ticks in whole milliseconds. The compiled render, model in, view and drawing, is 3.0 ms; the view alone, 1.6 ms. Both are near the Hazel test's (3.4 and 1.16 ms).

^report(45)'s ideas, here#

The Witch's compiled numbers draw the view's answer straight from its JS and never make it Hazel, as ^report(45) does. They use no diffs: a view's answer never comes back to Hazel, so there is nothing for a diff to carry. Matthew asked, on 2026-11-07 (this section added 20:30): are we using diffs, and are we skipping the trip of the Html back to Hazel?

  • The Html's trip back to Hazel: skipped, in every compiled time here. The view's answer is drawn straight from its JS (HazelJitDOM; ^report(45)'s a view's answer, never made Hazel, ^ticket(714)). It is made Hazel only if something asks for it (the lazy html of LivelitProj.eval_view_answer), and on this slide nothing does. The commands the view runs are answered in JS too. Until bcec455abc, the splice's value went back to Hazel and in again, and that was 20 of the 21 ms of the picture's drawing from JS in the Hazel test. ^report(45)'s cheaper ids are in as well, though a drawing from JS makes none.
  • Diffs: not used, and a render has nothing for one to carry. ^report(45)'s diff carries a new value back to Hazel against the last one (the way back as a diff, ^ticket(715)): in the app, the model a livelit's update returns. The picture's update returns its model unchanged (Pure(m)), and its model changes only when the program's run gives the splice a new value. The slide's sliders and polygon editors do run their updates compiled, their models back as diffs, but this report times views. What a render does carry whole is the other way: the picture into JS, 0.88 ms of the 3.4 ms frame in the Hazel test, each time the model changes. A diff that way is below.

In a real drag the slider follows the pointer before this, since a drag's input events only preview; this work comes when the change commits.
  • The run. The program then runs off the main thread, in Hazel's worker, and the picture is drawn when the run's value arrives. The status said the run went on until 5.8 to 11.9 s after the edit; on the arms edits, the picture changed before that.

Where a small edit's time goes#

The program's evaluation is 1.2 to 2.6 s of a small edit's 5.3 to 8.8 s. Most of the rest is the worker's answer: 4.4 to 5.4 MB for a 10 KB request, about 536 times its size. Beyond its evaluator, the worker takes 2.5 to 4.7 s before its result arrives. The main thread then decodes the result in 1.9 to 3.1 s, one long task during which the page cannot draw: at least 116 frames at 60 fps. (Matthew, 2026-11-07: "Yes, break it down".)

  • How it was timed. The page now keeps its own timeline when asked: Util.BenchMarks, on when globalThis.hazelBenchMarks is an array, and otherwise doing nothing. It records each update and calculate (with the statics inside it), the page's view, the work after display, the request posted to the worker with its size, and each message back, decoded and then handled. A result also brings the worker's own evaluator time. The check ran the same four edits each way with it on (3ed2f31844, run 42445268287); this time the picture showed the edit after 5.3 to 8.8 s.
  • Before the request: 0.18 to 0.27 s. The edit's update, one view of the page, and a calculate (the statics, and the request's pieces) run on the main thread; then the request goes, 10 KB. The page's first redraw comes after one more view.
  • The worker. On the neck edits, the picture comes with the run's result, 4.1 to 5.9 s after the request. The worker's evaluator ran 1.2 to 2.6 s of each run in all, so 2.5 to 4.7 s of the worker's time before its result went to something else. Making the result and encoding it is the likely part, but the worker's own split is not recorded yet. On the arms edits, the picture comes sooner, in a 4.4 MB stream message the worker sends while it runs, and the result follows seconds later.
  • Decoding on the main thread. A 4.4 MB stream took 1.3 to 1.5 s to decode, and a 5.4 MB result 1.9 to 3.1 s. Each is one task: while it runs, nothing on the page moves. On the arms edits that happens twice, the second time after the picture has already changed.

  • In all, the main thread was busy 2.1 to 3.6 s of the 5.3 to 8.8 s, most of it decoding; the rest of the time it waited for the worker.

Better numbers, from ^report(45)'s sharing#

After a small edit, the picture's view could redo only the shapes that changed. In Node that is 0.043 ms for the neck's 17 shapes and 0.071 ms for the arms' 30, against 0.24 ms for the whole view: 5 and 3 times less work, and 0.007 ms for one shape. But it needs the new picture to share its unchanged parts with the last one, and the sharing has to come from the trip in itself. Done after the trip in, as a walk of its own, it costs 0.23 ms: as much as the view it saves.

The idea is ^report(45)'s diff, run the other way. There, the diff stops wherever the last answer and the new one share an object, and the patch keeps every unchanged part of the old value. Here, the new picture is first merged with the last one, so that every part equal to the last one's is the last one's object. Then a view that keeps its per-shape work by the shape it was given redoes only the changed shapes. (The keeping is a memo by object identity, on the compiled shape_node and on shape_nodes' tail.) A prototype, data/witch/edit-share.mjs, takes hazel-jit's own compiled view and the slide's picture, moves the first vertex of k shapes spread through the picture, and times both ways, medians of 500 runs. What it suggests for hazel-jit#

Five changes to what hazel-jit writes, each something these compilers already do, would close most of the gap to ReScript. In the order they would likely help this rule:

  1. Pass a function's arguments as JS arguments. A Hazel function of a tuple, fun (n, i) -> …, is a JS function of one array, so every call allocates an array and reads it back. Where the parameter is a tuple pattern, compile (n, i) => … and call it with f(n, i), keeping the one-argument form for a function passed as a value.
  2. Compare Ints with ===. hazel-jit calls its generic structural equality for every ==; the elaborated term knows when both sides are Ints (or Floats, Bools, Strings).
  3. Turn tail calls into loops, as all three do for nth, pick and has: faster, and no stack to overflow on a long list.
  4. Unbox options, and make constructors flat. Some(t) as t and None as undefined saves an allocation per option; {TAG, _0, _1, _2} saves the inner array of {c, v: [a, b, k]}.
  5. Lay out labeled tuples as objects only, not as arrays with the labels as properties too: one shape per type, which JS engines optimize best.

On the Witch's picture the five matter less, since the builtin does most of the work there, but the first and fourth still apply: each point calls grow and grow_all with a fresh array of arguments, and each number goes through $R.b.to_fixed([x, 1]), another array, where the others call x.toFixed(1). A sixth change follows from it: call a builtin JS has directly when its arguments are known to be in range (to_fixed's digits a literal 1, say), as ReScript and Melange do.

Each changes the layout that the trip in and out reads and writes (^report(45)'s to_js and of_js), so each is a change to both sides, and to HazelJitDOM's reading of a view's answer. The first three change only the code inside a compiled function, and are the place to start.

Could any of them be used#

Not as hazel-jit's back end; but what they do, yes.

  • As a back end, each would need Hazel translated into its source language (ReScript or OCaml) and then compiled, in the browser, as the program changes. Hazel's holes, its gradual types and its livelits have no counterpart in either, so the translation would refuse much of what hazel-jit compiles, or fall back as hazel-jit does now. And the toolchain is large: Melange's playground compiles in the browser, but only by loading the whole compiler, built to JavaScript, first.
  • Their value layouts are a good target for hazel-jit's next version, above, and would let Hazel values pass to ReScript or Melange code directly, if Hazel apps ever share values with code written in them.
  • js_of_ocaml is what Hazel itself is built with, and its whole-program optimization (inlining across functions, as it did here) is not something a compiler working one definition at a time, in the browser, can do the same way.

Each alternative, for and against#

For running a Hazel program's views and updates compiled, in the browser, as the program is edited, hazel-jit stays the right base: it is the only one of these that compiles Hazel itself, all of it that it can, while the program runs. ReScript and Melange are the models for its output; js_of_ocaml stays what Hazel itself is built with. The question for each is the one Hazel has: compile a program that is being edited, holes and all, in the page, and hand its values back and forth with the editor.

hazel-jit#

  • For: it compiles Hazel's own elaborated terms, so nothing is translated first, and whatever it cannot compile (a hole, a test, a splice) runs on the interpreter, one definition or one form at a time. It runs in the page, and fast enough to compile on every edit: the Petri rule's program in about 0.6 ms, the Polygons livelit's definition in 3 to 10 ms. Its values come back as Hazel values, closures as Hazel closures, so the editor, the probes and the interpreter take them as their own. Its runtime is about 2.5 KB; the compiler is already in Hazel's bundle.
  • Against: its code is the slowest of the compilers here, 1.59 µs a call, about 2.9 times ReScript's, for the reasons above: arguments in an array, a generic equality, no loops, boxed options. It uses no types to choose its code yet. Its Ints are JS numbers, which overflow and round as JS's do, the best-case trade agreed for now. It compiles one definition at a time, so it cannot inline across a program as js_of_ocaml does. On the Witch's picture it is within 9% of ReScript, at 0.19 ms (5,200 fps).

ReScript#

  • For: among the fastest code here, 0.55 µs a call, and the most readable: named tags, objects by field name, functions called with their arguments. It is mature and used in production, its layouts are documented, and its code passes values to and from plain JS easily. On the Witch's picture, 0.18 ms (5,600 fps).
  • Against: it is a language of its own, with its own syntax and standard library, so Hazel would be translated into it first, and Hazel's holes, gradual types and livelits have no counterpart there: the translation would refuse an unfinished program, which is most programs while they are being edited. Its compiler is a native program; in the page it would have to be loaded as JavaScript, as its online playground does. And each edit would mean a translation and a build.

Melange#

  • For: the fastest compiled code here, 0.46 µs a call on the rule, 0.17 ms (5,800 fps) on the Witch's picture. It compiles OCaml, the language Hazel is written in, with OCaml's own tools (dune, opam), so Hazel's implementers know it, and OCaml that Hazel's tools generate could go through it unchanged. Its playground shows it running in the browser.
  • Against: everything said of ReScript as a back end, with OCaml in place of ReScript: Hazel translated to OCaml first, and OCaml's type checker rejecting what Hazel accepts (a hole, a mismatch the gradual types allow). The whole compiler loaded in the page. Its constructors are numbered, not named, so its JS is a little harder to read than ReScript's.

js_of_ocaml#

  • For: it already builds Hazel. It optimizes a whole program at once, and inlined the whole rule into its caller here (0.71 µs a call, a best case). It compiles OCaml's bytecode, so any OCaml runs, exactly as OCaml means it, its integers included.
  • Against: for a Hazel program it would mean Hazel to OCaml, OCaml to bytecode, bytecode to JS, ahead of time: far too heavy to do as a program is edited. Its JS is OCaml's memory layout (every value an array with its tag first), hard to read and awkward to hand to plain JS. And OCaml's library comes along, as JS: on the Witch's picture, numbers formatted with Printf make its view 5.3 times as slow as the same view calling JS's toFixed (1.01 against 0.19 ms).

BuckleScript#

  • For: it is where ReScript's and Melange's code generator comes from, and why their output looks alike.
  • Against: it is no longer developed under that name: ReScript continues it, and Melange is the fork that kept OCaml. Not an option of its own.

Writing the JS by hand#

  • For: no compiler at all, and full control: the Petri example's own fallback handler is written this way, for when its Hazel rules do not build.
  • Against: the point is to write the rule in Hazel, and see it in Hazel's editor. And hand-written JS is not the fastest by default: 0.90 µs a call here, slower than three of the compilers, because it is written the way people write JS. (On the Witch's picture, written with loops over arrays, it is the fastest: 0.17 ms.)

Hazel's interpreter#

  • For: all of Hazel: holes, tests, probes, stepping, the call memo, Hazel's own Ints. No compile step, and the semantics are the definition's.
  • Against: 358 µs a call on this rule, 225 times hazel-jit's; on a Polygons render, about 37 times (^report(45)); a frame of the Witch's picture, 155 ms (6 fps), 45 times hazel-jit's, and far outside a 60 fps frame. It stays what runs whatever hazel-jit cannot.
