Fewer, Deeper Fixes

^report(48) gathered every cost the series had measured in how Hazel runs a program and draws it, 196 of them, and called the whole "A Thousand Cuts". On 2026-11-09 Matthew Keenan answered it on Slack: fix them together, in a few well-chosen changes, not one at a time. Here are his message in full, Matthew Hammer's replies, and a response to each of his points from what the series measured. Matthew Hammer, 2026-11-10: "Let's write a report with this feedback, and our reasoned response to it."

In short#

We agree: a few well-chosen fixes, each written by hand and reviewed, are worth more than many separate ones, and our own record on the hazel-jit branch shows the risk he names. ^report(48)'s ledger files each of its 196 costs under one of 19 root causes. Six fixes would address the causes of 116 of them: Merkle hashes in the grammar, ids made cheap and kept temporary inside the evaluator, a value's type checked once, the worker as a language server, per-item statics landed on dev, and derived state and rendering through Bonsai. All six come from his message.

Wherever the series has numbers on one of his points, they bear out his gut reaction, with two refinements: a hash belongs on the node it describes, not in a side table, and a probe's environments are large only where they cross to the page, not in memory. The code the agent wrote stays a prototype. It answered fast whether a livelit's slider can show its step in tens of milliseconds, and which of the changes mattered; ^report(52) ranks them and orders a redo by hand in small increments.

Matthew Keenan's message#

His overall point: an agent fixing the costs it found one at a time would spread special-case logic through the codebase; a few well-chosen, well-implemented fixes and simplifications would do more in the long run. He sent it on Slack on 2026-11-09, in reply to ^report(48), as gut reactions rather than invariants, and asked to hear about any important facets he skipped (below). It is quoted as he wrote it.

my thoughts on your cuts that you can feed into your agent in case you want to inject more prompting/data/opinions into it. lmk (or get your agent to lmk) if there’s any important facets I skipped over that need a gut opinion.

note these are all gut reactions, I haven’t spent too long thinking about any particular one, please don’t treat any of them as invariants

Overall: I’m really really really scared of having an agent fix a thousand cuts it’s found. Especially I’m scared that it’ll fix them all separately. A lot of these can be fixed together. I’d say agent-generated prs, at least in 2026 still generally decrease the quality of the codebase rather than increasing it; a thousand independent fixes could go a long way to making the codebase harder to use; and I would think we could get more out in the long term if we do a few well-chosen well-implemented fixes and simplifications instead of a thousand things that only fix certain cases and plaster on special-case logic throughout the codebase

Ids-on-every-term:

  • Heavily in favor of any changes that are fully internal to Id.mk or id equality. The string thing sounds like it could be an easy, clean win. I’m pretty sure we’re still even using an old and possibly deprecated id library on ocaml.
  • Evaluation already doesn’t (or shouldn’t) mint new ids during evaluation. Evaluation does give new ids to output expressions, this is to make sure that output expressions don’t mess up tylr because tylr needs unique ids. Any changes to that need to be really careful not to re-introduce weird selection bugs when the ids are no longer uniqified coming out of evaluation. My preference would be to not let the temp ids escape evaluation, otherwise we have to think harder..
  • Ascriptions’ run-time checks look like they need a think-through. They shouldn’t be taking this long and if they are I worry Merklizaiton is just going to hide the problem, we probably want to make sure they’re not doing more checks than they should?.
  • List walking being quadratic sounds familiar - I’m pretty sure I’ve fixed that before, curious to know why it’s back - are those different scenarios or is it the same one back again? I don’t get why it should be quadratic in the length of the list..
  • ok after having seen a bunch of these being due to pushing list types, I’m thinking we should think seriously about bringing back something resembling the cast calculus, or at least find a way to remove casts/ascriptions in places where we know the types are safe. Could be a bit of a research question though. We removed casts originally because they were too confusing for maintainers; I suspect we can come up with something better..
  • variable lookups don’t need to mint new ids; in fact the evaluator ids really don’t matter because they’re replaced once the evaluator is finished. We should make sure there’s no new minting and everything new is Id.temp. That feels like it would be a pretty easy and principled change.
  • The evaluator shouldn’t be re-traversing anything that’s final and I’m not clear from your report where that’s coming from? It sounds like there could possibly be an issue where things coming out of the environment are getting evaluated - they shouldn’t (unless they’re a fixpoint), it should treat them as value, that’s what the final flag in transition is for.
  • The worker-editor transition is a massive problem, which I’m seeing in a load of different problems, but I don’t see the correct full fix. We either need to be able to send stuff over quickly, or just leave more information on the webworker and treat it as a language server. Any attempts to make what we’re sending over smaller I think are putting the cart before the horse..
  • Environments in probes is an interesting one: I kinda assumed that structure-sharing between environments would save most of the memory issues here, but I guess it doesn’t, worth now asking the quesiton then whether it’s fundamental: i.e. we are just storing that much memory because we want it on probes, or whether there’s a specific place hwere we’re leaking in terms of ocaml’s structure sharing..
  • Iirc Andrew had a branch where he was incrementalizing or memoizing all the statics and maketerm and everything for big programs - idk how it works or how well it went.
  • For lets, I wonder whether there’s a simpler version where we just always assume lets can be recursive, without having to do any of the fancy function checking or anything, might be more intuitive for the user to know they’re always recursive too. (Perhaps a warning if you just do let x = x, similar to the new type x = x warning).
  • Re-running the whole of statics is the incrementalizing statics issue. I think we’re letting perfect be the enemy of the good here, like we don’t have to make implementing Thomas’s statics as part of the global world tree be the first pass here..
  • Merkle hashing feels like a decently well-contained project (if engineered well) within grammar (or even just a ppx?) that could go a long way; especially on things like the expression comparisons in incremental, or type comparisons..
  • Incremental evaluation doesn’t do anything inside functions - I think that’s what the ‘incremental evaluation doesn’t work on sliders’ on is complaining about. With incremental evaluation we have to decide on some limit for what we’re caching. I was thinking we could do something with caching values by callstack ids, not just top-level id, and that’s what I described and proved in my paper - I think a names-based approach could be simpler here too though. I was hoping whatever we do we can still make it intuitive to the user what’s being cached and what’s not..
  • Undo keeping whole editor state is a known issue, I’d love to go bigger on using Bonsai to separate out the non-derived from derived information and only undoing non-derived information. I had a bonsaification branch a while ago which is probably archived now..
  • Rendering is again something I think can be fixed with better bonsaification, I think it has ways to make stuff render-on-demand and a better fine-grained system for re-rendering only parts that have changed.

Matthew Hammer's replies#

Matthew Hammer agreed that the agent's code lacks quality, and named what it is for: cheap prototypes, and quick answers to questions that would otherwise take days or weeks. He also apologized for the agent's wording. "Death by a thousand cuts" was his phrase for how expensive Hazel's operation is overall; the agent took to calling each cost a "cut", and then each improvement too. From this report on, a cost is a cost and an improvement is a change, and the earlier reports are being revised to match.

I’m not sure which PR you’re thinking about most, or maybe just in general, but I generally agree.

Six fixes#

Six fixes would address the causes of 116 of the 196 costs; the largest families they leave are the interpreter itself and the JS boundary. The figure files each cost under its root cause, as ^report(48)'s ledger does, and colors it by the fix that addresses that cause. "Addresses" is a judgment, not a measurement: a fix aimed at a cause need not remove every cost filed under it, and some costs are already fixed on one branch or another.

What the prototype is for#

The hazel-jit branch answered, in five days, whether a livelit's slider can show its step in the picture in tens of milliseconds. It can: 20 to 30 ms on the Witch slide, from 4.2 to 6.5 s. Its code measures what each change is worth; it is not code to land. That is the use Matthew Hammer names for agent-written code. ^report(52) ranks the ten changes that mattered by how much each helped and orders a redo by hand in nine increments, each its own pull request with its own tests. Our own record there is the risk Matthew Keenan describes: 120 commits on one branch, 6 of its 10 ingredients without a test of their own, 10 commits that only fixed the one before, and caches keyed on object identity, two of which made a drag slower before they were fixed.

^ticket(721), finished this morning as build 45, shows both sides. When a slider's step changes only the literals in a livelit's model, the item it edited is patched rather than analyzed again: a step's statics went from 19 to 53 ms on build 43 to 12 to 18 ms on build 45, and the edited item's part from 13 to 42 ms to 3 to 6 (one run each, on the Witch slide, below). That answers a question: finer-grained incremental statics would buy 10 to 35 ms on a slider step. It is also special-case logic: one kind of edit, patched by hand inside DefStatics, and a first attempt (build 44) sent the worker the whole program instead of the edited item. We propose keeping it as that measurement, and not landing it ahead of per-item statics on dev (fix 5).

What the message left out#

The root causes no fix here addresses: the interpreter itself and the JS boundary are the largest, 12 and 15 costs. Matthew Keenan asked to hear about facets he skipped that need a gut opinion; these are the ones the ledger has, each with what the series measured and the question we would put to him.

Root causeCostsWhat the series measuredThe question
interpretation itself12The interpreter is slow at what any interpreter is slow at: a livelit's render 40 times slower than compiled with the trip in and out, a recursive sum of 1,000 1,152 times (^report(45)).Should a compiler like hazel-jit become a second way to run Hazel programs, beside the interpreter, and if so who keeps the two in step? Or should it stay a livelit's fast path?
the JS boundary (js_of_ocaml)15js_of_ocaml's generic calls (11.6% of self time), values converted from JS as syntax the evaluator steps back into a value (56% of a call's floor), Uuidm's byte copy (^report(25)).Is there a representation of values that crosses the boundary cheaply, so that a JS call into Hazel does not rebuild what it was given?
environments and substitution8A value held in a closure is substituted into each call and stepped again (^report(25)); environment lookups 7.2% of a loop (^report(35)).Substitution or environments for the evaluator's own steps: is the final flag he mentions enough, or is it time to evaluate with environments throughout?
values are terms (open terms, holes, closures)7The Witch's picture sent as terms: 142.2 KB and 24.0 ms to encode and decode, against 48.0 KB and 6.09 ms as plain data (^report(47)).Should closed, first-order values (numbers, strings, lists, records of them) have a representation of their own, apart from terms?
persistent maps and allocation (GC)7Garbage collection 18% of a JS call's floor, 19.8% of a plain walk's self time (^report(25)); a pause of 20 to 35 ms once a slider step (^report(52)).Which of the evaluator's maps and copies could be mutable and local, or shared?
data representation (flat lists, no maps)4Hazel has no map, so a rule walks a list to find a square: 254 ms at 64x64, against 0.54 ms for Fumola's HashMap (^report(27)).A map in Hazel's library, or in the language?
parsing on load5Opening a program parses it whole (^report(48)'s five costs).Worth incremental parsing, or only a cache of parsed programs?
trampolines and stack safety4The evaluator's trampolines are part of a loop's per-pass cost (^report(29)).Is stack depth still the constraint that made them necessary?
scheduling (worker slices, message cadence)4How often the worker yields and sends (^report(4): 5.8 to 6.2 s of a 10 s change idle between slices, fixed).Folded into the language-server design (fix 4)?

What next#

  1. Agree as a team on the six fixes, or a different short list, and their order. Ids and per-item statics can start now.
  2. For ascriptions, count the checks per value on the World rule before caching anything, as he suggests.
  3. Find Matthew Keenan's bonsaification branch, and Andrew Blinn's notes on #2469, and measure each on one slide.
  4. Keep the hazel-jit branch as the measurement it is; redo its changes by hand in ^report(52)'s order, each only where it still helps once the deeper fixes land.
