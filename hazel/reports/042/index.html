1. Undo and redo in O(log n), in space and in time: how close are we, and why not yet? measured; the O(log n) design is open #1 #

Asked by Cyrus (2026-11-05), carried over by Matthew · ^ticket(665) · as of 2026-11-03, the last measurements

Matthew showed Cyrus the analysis of undo's cost on 2026-11-05 (^report(38)). The two questions continue: how do we get closer to O(log n) for an undo and a redo, in space and in time, and why are we not there yet, in each.

Time, where it stands. Every step grows in proportion to the program: on a program of n top-level definitions, an edit, an undo and a redo each have a log-log slope of 1.1 to 1.16 (^report(36)). Three fixes on #2635 made each step cheaper without changing that: the program's root id read from a cache (394552d5d0), no completions after a numeral (bfa36a6ede), and realizing the view after an undo no longer counted as an edit (9df1b2601e). Now a redo costs about an edit (41 ms against 46 at 3,200 definitions, dev build, one run), and an undo costs an edit plus a rebuild of the outline (85 ms).

Time, why not O(log n) yet. The incremental caches do their part: an edit, an undo or a redo re-parses one or two definitions and re-measures one or two chunks. What is linear is a handful of passes around them that walk every top-level definition: MakeTerm's top-level pass (about 38% of the profile at 3,200 definitions), DefStatics' walk of the chain of definitions (20%), the outline rebuilt from scratch on an undo (15%; only #2469's modular editors have it) and Measured's walk (6%). None of it is the undo history: pushing and popping an entry costs almost nothing. The top level is a list of definitions, so any pass over it, or any summary of it rebuilt after a change, is O(n). Not measured yet: evaluation and the view during an undo.

Space, where it stands. Dev keeps a whole copy of the editor for every edit, and caps the history only when a setting that is off by default is on (on #2597, Full Body kept 89 MB more per edit). Andrew Blinn's compaction (6010128b7d, on #2469 and #2635) drops each entry's derived data and keeps its zippers: then an entry adds about 0.01 MB at 400, 1,600 and 6,400 definitions, flat, because consecutive entries share nearly all their structure (^ticket(665), GitHub run 41695584813).

Space, why not O(log n) yet. Two parts are still linear. The newest entry of each stack is kept whole so that the latest undo is fast (032fe3aca6): 0.55, 1.9 and 6.9 MB at the three sizes, about 1.1 KB a definition. And what compaction drops (syntax, statics, evaluation) is rebuilt on a restore, so the space saved is paid in time. One caveat on the flat number: the probe typed into the same definition again and again; edits spread over the program may share less.

Toward O(log n), the candidates. (a) The top level as a balanced tree of definitions, each node with summaries of its subtree (the names it binds, its part of the skeleton, its rows): each pass touches only the edited definition's path, an entry shares every other node, and the worker's request after an edit shrinks the same way (^ticket(666) found a one-literal edit re-sends about 10% of a chain of lets, every let around the edit hashing anew). (b) Undo as an inverse edit applied to the live state, so derived data updates as it does for a keystroke and no snapshot needs rebuilding. (c) First, and cheapest: build the outline incrementally, which every undo pays for, and every edit while the sidebar is shown.

For the meeting: Cyrus's read of the two directions, a balanced tree of definitions or undo as an inverse edit, and which to prototype first. Is the incremental outline the right first step? For space: does flat-per-compacted-entry answer the question, or should the next measurement spread edits over the program, use a release build, or count the newest whole entry differently?

Started 2026-11-06, on a trial branch off #2635 (trial/694-view-jit, 79fe5bbebf). HazelJit.compile turns an elaborated term into a JS function, modules included. The Polygons slide's livelit compiles whole (no member left out, 24 KB of JS), and its view, applied to the model the slide's own shirt gives it, returns the same view command as the interpreter, its whole Html tree; 26 smaller programs agree too, the Petri net rule of ^report(43) among them. Breaking the compiler on purpose fails the tests, so the comparison is real. First numbers (Node, a dev build, medians of 30, two runs): the view's program takes 12 to 17 ms on the interpreter and 0.06 to 0.09 ms compiled, 140 to 300 times faster, after 2 ms to compile. Not counted yet: carrying the model into JS and the command tree back, and the call memo the app uses. Next: that boundary (an event handler in the Html has to come back as a Hazel function), the setting, and timings in browsers.

The idea, in Matthew's words (2026-11-06): Reason code, called hazel-jit, that compiles some of a Hazel program's syntax tree into JavaScript that does mostly the same thing, and calls that instead of the interpreter. Messed Up Dress Up already has three kinds of handler for the same rule: the game's own JS, a Hazel function and a Fumola one, each written by hand. hazel-jit would be a general way to replace slow Hazel subroutines with JS made from them.

The best case first. Numbers need not match exactly for now: overflow, rounding and the like may differ between Hazel's numbers and JS's. The question is how much faster JS could make some Hazel subroutines at best.

What to compile first: the livelit view functions on the slides, especially those that draw many user-defined polygons. In the Livelit Playground deck: Polygons and Polygon Viewer, then Full Body (2,929 lines, the most polygons), the Witch, Hands, and the Scenes slide's woods.

The design so far (^ticket(694), 2026-11-05): hazel-jit turns an elaborated term into JS source and runs it with new Function. It covers variables, functions and recursive functions with their closures, application, let, match and its patterns, if, tuples and labels, lists (as cons cells, so :: stays constant time), literals, operators, constructors, and builtins (JS versions of the common ones, the rest through a bridge to the OCaml builtin). Types, ascriptions and parentheses are erased. Anything else (holes, tests, filters, projectors, splices) leaves the view on the interpreter, with the reason kept. Only a view and what its closure reaches are compiled, not update, init, expand or the program. The model goes in as JS and the command tree comes back as a Hazel value, so the drawing is unchanged. A setting, jit_views, on by default. Lost while compiled: the call memo's sharing between renders, probes inside the view, and the step budget.

Timings: for each slide, the interpreter's time, the compile time, and the compiled view's time with the boundary crossing, as medians with ranges, in Node first, then in browsers on GitHub's machines.

For the meeting: Is the best-case framing right, numbers allowed to differ? Is compiling the view alone the right first cut? What would make a convincing comparison: which slides, and against what (the interpreter with its call memo, or the game's hand-written JS)?

Update, 11-07: compared, in ^report(46) (^ticket(698)). ^report(43)'s Petri rule, written in ReScript and in OCaml, compiled by ReScript 12.3.1, Melange 5.0.0 and js_of_ocaml 5.9.1, beside hazel-jit's output and a hand-written handler, each listing in the report. A call, in Node: Melange 0.46 µs, ReScript 0.55, js_of_ocaml 0.71, by hand 0.90, hazel-jit 1.59, the interpreter 358. ReScript's and Melange's code reads like the hand-written: a function's arguments as JS arguments, === on ints, loops for tail calls, options without a box, records as objects. Those are ^report(46)'s five suggestions for hazel-jit, and each section of its end weighs one route: hazel-jit as it is, ReScript or Melange as a back end, js_of_ocaml, by hand, the interpreter.

Cyrus named BuckleScript and ReScript as technologies to know about, and perhaps compare with, if not use. What they are, briefly, to be checked as part of ^ticket(698): BuckleScript, from Bloomberg (2016), compiled OCaml to readable JavaScript. Its team later renamed it ReScript, with a syntax of its own, aimed at the JavaScript world (version 12 in 2026). Melange is a fork that kept OCaml and Reason syntax, with first-class support in dune. Hazel itself is compiled to JS by js_of_ocaml, which works from OCaml's bytecode: a different route.

Three ways they bear on hazel-jit: (1) a reference for its output, since they map a typed functional language's values to JS (variants, records, lists, closures, functions of several arguments) and are known for fast, readable output; (2) a route to use: turn Hazel into OCaml, Reason or ReScript and let their compiler make the JS, ahead of time, or in the page if their compiler runs there; (3) Hazel's own build: what Melange's output would do to the speed of Hazel's evaluator, against js_of_ocaml's, a larger question.

For the meeting: Which did Cyrus have in mind: a reference for hazel-jit's output, a tool to use, or a different compiler for Hazel itself?
