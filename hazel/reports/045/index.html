hazel-jit: Livelit Views Compiled to JavaScript

How much faster can a livelit's view run as JavaScript compiled from its Hazel, instead of on Hazel's interpreter? Decided at the meeting with Cyrus on 2026-11-05, as the best case first: JS's numbers allowed to differ from Hazel's for now (^ticket(694)). This report follows the work as it goes, for the next meeting on Wednesday 2026-11-11 at 10:00 Mountain, where it is item 2 of the agenda, ^report(42).

Outline#

  1. How much faster: a render of the Polygons slide's view, on the interpreter and compiled, its first renders and once warm.
  2. Where the time goes now: the trip back, the view's answer made again as Hazel terms, is most of a compiled render; the trip in is small.
  3. Three ideas for the way back (Matthew, 2026-11-07): cheaper ids, a view's answer never made Hazel, and the way back as a diff; all three work, and not coming back at all helps most.
  4. How it works: the compiler, how Hazel's values look in JS, and the trip in and out.
  5. In the app: where the compiled view runs, the setting, and what happens when it cannot.
  6. Does it give the same answers: the tests, and checking that they can fail.
  7. What it does not do yet.
  8. For Wednesday's meeting: the agenda's questions about this work.
  9. What next, and how this was put together.

How much faster#

A render of the Polygons slide's view takes 13.2 ms on the interpreter and 0.32 ms compiled, about 41 times faster, counting the trip into JS and back, once Node has warmed up to it. The first 30 renders after compiling take 0.66 ms (their median), about 20 times faster: the first took 2.13 ms, and they fall to about 0.35 ms while Node optimizes the way back. The compiled code itself takes 0.043 ms of that, about 309 times faster than the interpreter. Smaller programs gain more, since there is no trip to take: the Petri net rule of ^report(43), answering four events, 7.74 ms against 0.016 ms (487 times), and a recursive sum of 1,000 numbers, 49.0 ms against 0.038 ms (1,292 times).

Update, 2026-11-06 19:00: the first version of this report said a render was about 16 times faster. Those renders were timed first and cold, the first 30 after compiling, and their times fell from 2.1 ms to 0.4 ms over the 30 runs as Node optimized the way back, so their median measured the warm-up more than a render. Each series is now timed after 10 untimed runs. Warm, a render was then about 34 times faster; the first renders, timed cold as before, are a row of their own (^ticket(694)).

These are Node's numbers, from a development build, on one laptop, medians of 30 runs in one test session; the runs vary (the Petri net rule's interpreter time ran from 4.8 to 9.2 ms over the day's test sessions), so read the ratios to within a factor of two. The interpreter's numbers do not include the call memo the app gives a view (^report(1)'s CallMemo), which saves calls repeated between renders; compiling the definition took 5.66 ms, once. Browser numbers, and a release build, come next.

Where the time goes now#

The trip back is 84% of a compiled render: 0.27 ms of 0.32. The trip in takes 0.033 ms (10%) and the compiled view 0.043 ms (13%), so the way back costs about 8 times the way in, and about 6 times the view itself. Each step was timed alone, warm, and the three add up to 0.34 ms, about the whole render's time.

Why the way back costs more: there is more of it. The compiled view needs its model as JS values, so the model is converted on the way in; and the app draws a Hazel value, so the view's whole answer, its command and every node of its Html tree, is made again as a Hazel term on the way out. The slide's model is 115 nodes (cons cells, tuples, constructors and the numbers and strings in them); the answer is 861, about 7.5 times as many, with 7 event handlers, none of them capturing a value. Per node, the way back costs a little more: 0.31 µs a node, against 0.28 µs a node in.

Making ids is now 17% of the way back; yesterday it was almost half. Each Hazel term carries an id, and each term the way back makes gets a fresh one. The trip back makes 906 terms; 906 fresh ids now take 0.045 ms (0.05 µs each), and with no ids to make at all, every term given one shared id, the trip back would take 0.20 ms instead of 0.27. How, under cheaper ids.

Update, 2026-11-07 07:40: this paragraph said each fresh id was a random UUID. It is not: Id.mk was already a counter, with a random tail drawn once per session. What cost was building each id's 16 characters out of eight concatenations; that is what changed.

What else could make the way back cheaper: keep the answer in JS until it is drawn, so the part of the app that turns it into the page's elements reads JS values directly; convert lazily, only the parts that are read; or keep what did not change since the last render. The first and the last are tried below.

Three ideas for the way back#

All three work, and the one that helps most is not coming back at all. Matthew asked, on 2026-11-07: can the ids be cheaper; can a view's answer, which is only drawn, skip Hazel altogether; and can the answer come back as a diff against the last one? Cheaper ids are done, in Id.mk itself. A view's answer drawn straight from its JS makes the same page in about a third of the time, and a whole render, model to page, is then about 40 times faster than on the interpreter. A diff carries one changed point of 1,000 back 87 times faster than carrying the whole list. What is not there yet is a diff applied in time sublinear in a long list: that needs lists to be trees on both sides (^ticket(710)).

Cheaper ids: done#

Making 906 ids now takes 0.045 ms; made as before, in the same session, 0.11 ms (between 2.5 and 6 times as long over today's runs). An id is 16 bytes: a counter in the first eight, then eight random bytes drawn once per session. Under js_of_ocaml a string is a JS string, and Id.mk built each id by concatenating one-byte strings, eight concatenations an id. Bytes 1 to 15 are the same for 256 ids in a row, so now they are made once for each 256, and each id is one concatenation. The ids are the same as before, byte for byte. Nothing downstream of the way back reads the ids of a view's answer; the call memo already puts its keys in a form with no ids.

This is not hazel-jit's alone. The interpreter mints an id for every term it rebuilds and every binding it makes, hundreds of thousands a run, through the same Id.mk. The string-built ids came in with a commit of 2026-10-10 on #2635's line (7a5667e6f2), which dev does not have yet.

A view's answer, never made Hazel#

Yes: drawn straight from its JS, a view's answer makes the same virtual DOM in 0.27 ms, against 0.75 ms through Hazel. Through Hazel, the answer is carried back (0.27 ms), its command run down to its Html (0.0008 ms), and the Html drawn by HazelDOM into the virtual DOM (0.46 ms), which costs more than the trip back. A prototype, HazelJitDOM, reads the compiled view's JS answer and makes the virtual DOM nodes directly. Model to page, a render is then 0.34 ms, against 0.82 ms through Hazel and 13.6 ms on the interpreter.

  • The same page. The two drawings, made into virtual-dom's own objects, are equal field by field (10,861 characters of them as JSON), event handlers compared only as functions. A quick test checks it.
  • Handlers cross only when their event comes. A click's message is carried back as Hazel when the click comes, not on every render. A handler that takes a payload (a mouse position, an input's text) is the compiled JS function: it is called in JS on the payload, and only the message it answers is carried back. Through Hazel, the interpreter applies the Hazel handler instead.
  • What it covers. The forms the Polygons view uses: the command Pure; text, inputs, custom and SVG elements, and the elements that take attributes and children; styles, sizes, values and raw attributes; clicks, mouse positions and input. Any other form is refused, and that render goes through Hazel as before. Splices, the editor commands and apps (which commit to the program's syntax, not to a model) are not covered.
  • Not in the app yet. eval_view would hand back the JS answer with the types to read its messages by, and the three places that draw a livelit's view would draw it: the optimistic render after a drag, the live samples, and eval_view's own.

The way back as a diff (^ticket(709))#

A diff of the last answer and the new one, applied to the last Hazel value, carries one changed point of 1,000 back in 0.064 ms, against 5.58 ms whole. The diff is computed in JS and stops wherever the two answers share an object: compiled code builds its answer functionally, so what it did not touch (a list's tail after the element it replaced, a field it did not set) is the same object as before. The patch keeps every part of the old Hazel value that did not change: in the test, 999 of the 1,000 elements are the old ones.

How often is it worth it? For a view, now, never: its answer need not come back at all. What must come back is what lives in the program: the model a livelit's update returns, and the answer of any compiled function the interpreter calls. On today's slides those are small: the Polygons model is 115 nodes, and carrying it in takes 0.033 ms. Carrying back costs about 0.3 µs a node for the Polygons view's answer, and about 5.58 µs a point for a list of labeled points with no type to read them by, so a whole list of 1,000 points is 5.58 ms of a 16.7 ms frame. A diff pays once a model holds hundreds of elements: a drawing with many points, a table, a long document.

The diff language is small. A diff is one of:

  • the same: nothing to carry;
  • new: a value carried back whole, at the type expected there;
  • a constructor's argument, changed by a diff;
  • a tuple's components, those that changed, each by a diff;
  • a list's edits, in order from its head: keep the next n elements, change the next by a diff, insert one, drop one, append a list at the end, or end here. After the last edit, the rest is the old list's own tail.

The common cases, each a quick test that patching gives what carrying back whole gives: one element changed in the middle; one put at the head; the head dropped; one put at the end; a list cut short; a list emptied; nothing changed; a constructor's argument changed; one labeled field set and the other kept; floats in a list. Putting one at the head, or dropping the head, is found without looking at the rest: the new list's tail is the old list, or the other way round.

Sublinear, not yet. The diff and the patch both walk a list from its head to the change. Each step is cheap (a few nanoseconds to compare two objects in JS, a few tens to keep an element in OCaml), so one change of 10,000 points costs 0.17 ms, but it grows with the change's position, not with the size of the change. Time in log n needs a long list to be a tree on both sides of the boundary: in the compiled code, instead of cons cells, and in Hazel's values, instead of a list literal's flat list. Adapton-style level trees, as in Fumola, give every list one shape however it was built, so a change touches one path. That is a change to how Hazel represents lists: ^ticket(710), for a talk first.

Two costs found on the way back, and fixed. A whole number came back through Printf and big-integer parsing, about 2 µs each; one that fits an int is now converted directly. And a tuple's labels were told from its indices by raising and catching an exception for each label. With both, a list of 1,000 labeled points carries back whole in 2.2 to 5.6 ms over today's runs, against 8.2 ms before.

How it works#

hazel-jit turns an elaborated Hazel term into the source text of a JS function, once, and the browser's own JavaScript engine runs it. HazelJit.compile_full, in src/language/dynamics/HazelJit.re, walks the term Hazel's elaborator makes (the same term the interpreter runs) and writes JS, which is made into a function with new Function.

Hazel's values in JS. Numbers are JS numbers (so Int overflow and rounding are JS's: the best case first); a list is cons cells ({h, t}, with null for the empty list), so :: takes constant time; a tuple is a JS array, each labeled component also a property by its label, so p.x is a property read; a constructor is {c: "Name", v: payload}; a function is a JS function of one argument; a module is a labeled value of its members, in order. A let or a case binds JS variables declared at the top of the enclosing function, each with a name of its own, so the code is expressions all the way down and no closure is made for a let. Types, ascriptions and parentheses are erased. Builtins written in OCaml have JS versions by name; builtins written in Hazel are compiled once, before the program's code.

What it does not compile, it says why, and that code runs on the interpreter: holes, tests, quotations and splices, projectors, partial application. A member of a module that does not compile (a livelit's expand, say) becomes a stub that says why if it is called, so the rest of the module still compiles.

The trip in and out (HazelJitValue.re). Into JS is direct: each Hazel value as above. Back is harder, in two ways. A JS number does not say whether it was an Int or a Float. So the compiler records each constructor's type as the elaborator gave it, and the type aliases the term defines, and the way back reads a number by the type expected where it sits: a constructor's argument at its argument's type, through aliases. A whole number with no type to go by comes back an Int. A function in the answer, an event handler like Attr.on_input(fun v -> SetName(v)), has to come back as a Hazel function the interpreter can apply when the event comes. So every compiled function carries the index of the Hazel function it was compiled from and the values it captured, and comes back as that Hazel function closed over those values (the compiler notes, for each function, which outer variables its body uses).

In the app#

Livelits' views run compiled in the app, behind a setting that is on by default, and the interpreter runs any view hazel-jit cannot. LivelitProj.eval_view compiles each livelit definition once (kept by its term), calls its compiled view on the model, and hands the command it returns to the code that runs view commands and draws their Html, as before. When the compiled view answers, the definition is not evaluated by the interpreter at all on that render.

  • The setting is Compiled views, in the settings menu, on by default; off, every view runs on the interpreter as before.
  • When it cannot (a form it does not compile, a recursive function in the answer, an error while running), the view runs on the interpreter, the browser's console says why, once for each reason, and that definition is not tried again.
  • Not checked in a browser yet. The tests run the same code in Node; trying it on the slides is the next step.

Does it give the same answers#

Yes, on every program tested, and the tests catch a broken compiler. test/Test_HazelJit.re has 37 tests, all passing at 7a7151443c: 24 small programs whose compiled results agree with the interpreter's (arithmetic, floats, recursion, tuples and labels, lists, constructors, options, strings, equality, closures, nested patterns, modules, builtins, and the Petri net rule); the Polygons slide's view, its whole Html tree equal to the interpreter's; values carried back as the interpreter's own (whole floats staying floats, a type alias, labels with no type, the Petri net's commands); a view and an event handler through the trip in and out, the handler then applied by the interpreter; and the Polygons view through it. Breaking the compiler on purpose fails them: compiling Int's + as - failed 5 of 18 programs; reading numbers without their types failed 3 tests; dropping the values a function captures failed the handler test.

What it does not do yet#

  • Numbers are JS's. Int overflow, division and rounding may differ from Hazel's: allowed for the best case, to be decided before anything ships.
  • The call memo, probes and the step budget do not apply inside a compiled view: no reuse of repeated calls between renders, no probe's samples from inside the view, and a deep recursion is JS's stack, not Hazel's step limit (a stack overflow falls back to the interpreter).
  • A recursive function in a view's answer is refused (a Hazel closure cannot hold a cycle as a plain value), and so is a JS builtin passed as a handler.
  • Values made again on the way back get new ids; whether anything in the app depends on a model's ids surviving the trip has not been checked.
  • The direct drawing and the diff are prototypes. Neither is called by the app yet; the drawing covers the forms the Polygons view uses, and the diff has been tried on lists of points and the ten small cases.
  • One slide measured. Full Body, the Witch, Hands and the Scenes slide's woods, the slides with the most polygons, come next.
