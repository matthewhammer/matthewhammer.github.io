What Mattered, Ranked

From 2026-11-06 to 2026-11-10 we made a livelit's slider in Hazel fast enough to drag. Of everything we changed, what made the difference, how much did each change help, and in what order could the team redo it by hand, in small steps? Matthew, 2026-11-10: "a new report that summarizes our work over the last several days, with plots of the current status, and the more significant ingredients, not every single thing we tried in order. Rank them by how much they ended up helping." The full record, build by build, is in ^report(50) and ^report(51); this report keeps only what mattered.

Outline#

  1. Where it stands: a slider's step on the Witch slide shows in the picture in about 20 to 30 ms, a drag runs at 55 to 58 frames a second, and the page still does 170 to 180 ms of its own work after each step.
  2. The ingredients, ranked: ten changes, ranked by the time each took off when it landed, in two groups: what a user waits for, and the page's own work after the picture.
  3. What is left: the edit made after the paint, the worker's answer two seconds later, and a pause once a step.
  4. Redoing it by hand: an order of increments, each small enough to review, with its size, its tests and what it needs first.
  5. The builds this report cites, what next, and how this was put together.

Where it stands#

On build 38, a step of the Witch's neck slider shows in the picture 20 to 31 ms after the click (78 ms for a slide's first step), a 3-second drag runs at 55.7 frames a second, its view 6.3 ms a frame, and the page's own work after each step's picture adds up to 167 to 183 ms. On 11-08, before any of this, a step took 4.2 to 6.5 s to show, and a drag could not move the picture at all. The Witch slide is the Livelit Playground's largest: 2,222 lines of Hazel and 53 livelits, a picture of a witch drawn from polygons and posed by sliders. Every number here is from the same check in Firefox on GitHub's machines, one run per build. Builds 41 and 43, the newest, ran on slower machines; build 43: 21 to 28 ms, 55.3 fps, 192 to 211 ms.

A step is now four kinds of work. Before the picture, the slide's compiled run answers the slider's event in a few milliseconds and the page draws the livelits whose answers changed. After it, in the frames that follow, the page writes the new model into the program, the edit (73 ms here: the update, the statics, the syntax and a view), and builds the sidebar's outline (14 ms). About a second later the slide autosaves. And at 1.38 s the worker's answer arrives, the whole program run on Hazel's interpreter, and the page checks it against what it has drawn (80 ms).

The ingredients, ranked#

Two changes did most of it: sending the worker's answer without what the page did not read (2.8 to 4.8 s off a step, when it landed), and compiling the slide's whole run to JavaScript (1.1 to 1.5 s more, and the reason a step shows in about 20 ms today). Of the page's own work after the picture, keeping the livelits a change did not touch helped most, 0.19 to 0.40 s a step. The ranking is by the time each change took off when it landed, on the measure it changed, as ^report(45) to ^report(51) measured it. The measures differ: a step's picture is what a user waits for after a click; a drag's frame is what they see while dragging, 60 times a second; the page's work after the picture is what can make the next frame late. So the ranking is in two groups, and a gain in one is not added to a gain in the other.

Three things to keep in mind when reading it. First, a gain can shrink once another lands: the worker's answer was the step's whole wait until the run was compiled, and now it arrives in the background. Second, the ranges are over a build's steps and over one run each; machines differ by up to a third from run to run (^report(50) ran build 7 three times: 26.2, 26.4 and 34.3 fps). Third, the drag's 80 to 85 ms is per frame, at up to 60 frames a second, so for a drag it matters more than its place in the list.

What is left#

The largest piece of a step's work left is the edit made after the paint, 75 to 90 ms on a fast machine, and it is under way as ^ticket(721). Build 42 timed the edit's parts: the zipper's own action (writing the model's text into the program) 11 to 20 ms; the statics 14 to 35 ms, of which re-analyzing the edited top-level item is 12 to 28 and parsing 2 to 8; the syntax cache 11 to 29; and the view after it 25 to 38. The zipper's action looked for the livelit's projector by walking every one of the program's 43,171 pieces; build 43 walks only the pieces whose measured extent holds it, and the action takes 4 to 6 ms. A model can change the program's values but not its types or its outline, so the statics need not be worked out again; but keeping them is not a matter of skipping a step. A livelit reads its own model from its use's statics record, which holds the use's term, so that record, its elaborated term and the program's elaboration, which the worker runs, would all need the new model, and every record above it holds a term with the old one. Build 44 (1ea585bea8 to d90a8cf135, on a branch of its own) does that: a commit that changes only a model's literals keeps the model's ids, and the statics are patched along the path from each literal up, in 0 to 1 ms instead of 14 to 35. But the worker's request is sent in pieces split along the statics' own per-item analysis (DefStatics), and finding none for the patched statics it went whole, about 70 ms more on the page. So the patch belongs inside that analysis: the edited item patched, and the program put back together by its own incremental step, so what is sent to the worker matches. Build 45 (9ae518ffe7) does that, and the edited item is patched at every step, none refused: a step's statics take 12 to 18 ms, from 19 to 53 on build 43; the edited item's part 3 to 6 ms, from 13 to 42; and the worker is sent the edited item again, 9 to 10 KB (one run each). It is also the kind of special case Matthew Keenan warns about; ^report(53) proposes keeping it as a measurement of what finer-grained statics would buy, and landing per-item statics on dev first.

After that: the worker's answer, which still runs the whole program on the interpreter 1.5 to 2.4 s after a step and costs the page 50 to 60 ms to take in, though the compiled run already answered (^report(50) step 4, partly done); a pause of 20 to 35 ms once a step after the answer, landing somewhere else each time, most likely the browser collecting the answer's garbage; and the step itself, 20 to 30 ms, a frame or so above 60 fps's 16.7.

Redoing it by hand, in small increments#

The work can be redone in nine increments, ruler first, each a pull request small enough to read, and five of the ten ingredients need neither hazel-jit nor each other. There is a feeling on the team that some of this work should be redone by hand, to avoid the debt of machine-written code, in small increments. Some facts about what there is to redo: the branch, trial/694-view-jit, has 120 commits from 11-06 to 11-10, adding 9,200 lines to src/ and removing 1,086, in 53 files, with 2,422 lines of tests and 672 of scripts and CI. The compiler is 2,975 of those lines, in four modules, and 2,063 of the test lines check that each compiled program agrees with the interpreter. Of the ten ingredients, 6 came with no test of their own; the Firefox check, which drives the app and times it, was their only check. And 7 commits only fixed the one before, which did not compile, since nothing was compiled on the laptop and every type check ran on GitHub.

The order below puts the measuring first, then what helps any branch, then hazel-jit's pieces in the order they depend on each other. Each increment names the ingredient it carries; its size is the lines that ingredient's commits added to src/, a ceiling, since they include the timers each change was measured with.

What next#

  1. ^ticket(721) is done as a measurement (build 45): patching the edited item instead of analyzing it takes 10 to 35 ms off a step's statics. Whether any of it lands waits on per-item statics on dev (^report(53)).
  2. Skip the worker's run when the compiled run has answered, or take its answer in without a whole view.
  3. Agree with the team on the order of the hand redo, and which increments go to dev first.

How this was put together#

The numbers are the ones ^report(45) to ^report(51) measured, cited by build. The figures are drawn from each build's run of the Firefox check (scripts/jit_slides.py in jit.yml on GitHub's machines): its jit.json and jit.log, kept in ^report(50)'s and 051's data/runs/. A step's time is from the slider's click to the picture changing, steps 2 to 4 of the four (the first step of a slide pays first-call costs); the page's work after the picture is the sum of its top-level marks (view, calculate, update, decode, the outline, the comparison) from the picture to half a second after the worker's answer; a drag is 3 seconds of the neck slider. Each ingredient's code size is git show --numstat over the commits the ranked table names, in ~/hazel-694-jit, counting src/ without hazel-jit's own four modules (counted apart), and test/. The generator is 052/gen052.py; the numbers are in 052/data/numbers.json and in the page.
